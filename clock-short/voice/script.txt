Until eighteen forty-seven, nobody in Britain agreed on what time it was.
Every town kept its own time, set by when the sun was highest over its own rooftops.
When it was noon in London, it was only ten to twelve in Bristol.
That didn't matter much when the fastest thing on the road was a horse.
Then the railways arrived, and the trains didn't care about your sunshine.
A timetable said the train leaves at nine, but nine where? London nine, or the station clock nine?
People ran for trains that had left, and waited for trains that were never coming.
So one railway company said, fine. Every clock on our line shows London time.
Station by station, the other companies gave in, until nearly every railway ran on one time.
Bristol did something very polite about it. Its famous clock grew a second minute hand.
One hand for the sun, and one for the train.
Imagine ten drummers who each hear their own beat. Nothing can march together until they follow one drum.
Britain only made it the law in eighteen eighty, but the trains had already decided.
So the next time your phone shows the exact same time as everyone else's, thank the railway.
And Bristol, which still keeps both hands on the wall, just in case.
